# A3 REFERENCES. Unobvious sources for The Charter Office.

Scope note. Two other agents hold banknotes and security printing technique. Nothing
here is a banknote and nothing here is an engraving method. Every entry below is
office paper: paper produced by a registry, a notary, a booking clerk, a dock, a
post office counter or a university bursar. Each one survived the test "would this
appear in a roundup of pretty old money". Anything that would have appeared there
was cut before it reached this file.

Fourteen references. Ranked summary and the structural argument are at the bottom
under CHIEF READ THIS FIRST.

## 1. Papel sellado, Spanish crown stamped paper

Issuer: Spanish crown, later the Mexican Republic
Date: instituted in Spain 1636, in the Indies soon after; the sheet described carries
seals for 1806-07, 1810-11, 1820-21 and 1824-25
Link: https://www.historians.org/perspectives-article/papel-sellado/

What it is. A monopoly paper stock. You could not write a will, a contract, a
petition or a viceregal edict on plain paper; you had to buy a sheet the crown had
already stamped, and the class of sheet you bought was set by what you were writing.
The stamp was on the paper before a single word of the document existed.

Technique or layout move. The seals descend down the left margin of the sheet rather
than sitting at the head. When a sheet went unused into a new biennium it was not
thrown away, it was restamped, so a single surviving sheet carries four successive
crowned medallions stacked down the left edge across nineteen years, each one
a different die. The written text begins to the right of that column and respects it
as a hard gutter. Third class paper cost two reales; a viceregal dispatch took a
twenty four real sheet; paper for the poor cost a quarter real. The price of the
blank governed the seriousness of the document written on it.

WHAT WE TAKE. A left margin column, roughly 90px wide, holding a vertical stack of
small stamp blocks that belong to the paper and not to the certificate. The
certificate body starts to the right of it and never crosses it. One block is the
current issue and carries the file number; the blocks above it are prior issues of
the same stock, printed at 25 percent opacity in the second colour. This gives us a
sheet with a history that predates the user's string, which is exactly the feeling
we want and costs nothing to render.

WHAT WE DISCARD. Crowns, coats of arms, the eagle and serpent, and any price in
reales. No heraldry anywhere on this project.

Rights. American Historical Association Perspectives article, editorial use. Read for
the technique, do not reproduce the image.

## 2. Impressed duty stamps, albino embossing and the tin strip patch

Issuer: UK Board of Commissioners of Stamps and successors across the Commonwealth
Date: British stamp duty from 1694; catalogued examples 1879 Victoria, circa 1907
Western Australia, 1933 South Africa, 1946 India, 1959 Ireland
Links: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impressed_duty_stamp
https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Embossed_duty_stamps

What it is. Tax paid by pressing a metal die directly into the document. Not a stuck
on label, a deformation of the sheet. Documents without the impression were
unenforceable in court, so the mark is a validity condition rather than decoration.

Technique or layout move. Two moves, and the second is the good one. First, early
impressions were albino, struck with no ink at all, so the mark reads only as raised
and depressed paper. Second, the die could not be struck into vellum, so for vellum
documents the stamp was impressed onto a small separate slip of paper, and that slip
was then fixed to the document with glue and a thin tin metal strip. The result is a
patch: a different paper, a different colour, a raised die, and a visible metal
fastener holding it down, sitting on top of the main sheet.

WHAT WE TAKE. Both. An ink free seal rendered purely as a shadow and a highlight,
zero fill, so it reads as pressure not print. Then, separately, one applied patch in
the lower left of the certificate, drawn as a slightly different paper tone with its
own edge shadow and two small dark fasteners at top and bottom. It says this mark was
added later, by a different office, to a document that already existed. That is
precisely the relationship between Window 1 and Window 2.

Note for Chief. One of the freely licensed Commons files is literally a 1933 Standard
Bank of South Africa cheque carrying an impressed duty stamp. The coincidence with
our working directory is worth a private smile and nothing else. Do not use the image.

WHAT WE DISCARD. Denominations, the word duty, and any suggestion a fee was paid.
Our paper is free and says so.

Rights. Commons structured data CC0, text CC BY-SA. Technique only, no image reuse.

## 3. Chirograph and indenture, the wavy cut through a written word

Issuer: English chancery and private parties, standard practice
Date: medieval, in wide use from the twelfth century
Links: https://www.nottingham.ac.uk/manuscriptsandspecialcollections/researchguidance/medievaldocuments/authentication.aspx
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chirograph

What it is. One agreement written two or three times on a single skin. The word
CYROGRAPHUM was then written large in the empty band between the copies, and the
skin was cut in half straight through that word. Each party walked away with a half.

Technique or layout move. The authentication is the cut. The two halves prove each
other by fitting, and the letters of the word split across the join make a forged
match impossible. The cut is deliberately wavy or spiky rather than straight, so the
edge itself is data. The University of Nottingham guide notes the resulting document
has a characteristic wavy or spiky top edge, which is how you identify an indenture
in a box of flat charters before you read a word of it.

WHAT WE TAKE. This is the strongest idea in the file. Our certificate is one half of
a chirograph. Its top edge is not straight, it is a low amplitude wave rendered as an
SVG path clip, and a line of text runs along that edge and is cut by it, so the top
third of each letterform is missing. The copy on that line is the office's own words,
not the user's. The reader instantly understands there is a counterpart somewhere
with the top halves of those letters on it, held by whoever keeps the register.

WHAT WE DISCARD. Latin, parchment texture, and any actual duplication. We show one
half and never the other.

Rights. Nottingham guide is a public research guide, cite it. Wikipedia CC BY-SA.

## 4. The plica, and seals attached by tag rather than printed

Issuer: general chancery and deed practice
Date: medieval onward
Link: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plica_(sigillography)

What it is. The bottom edge of a charter is folded upward to double the parchment's
thickness. Slots are cut through both layers, a parchment tag or cord is threaded
through, and the seal hangs off the tag. The fold exists solely so the seal does not
tear its way out of the sheet.

Technique or layout move. The seal is not on the document, it is through it. The
sheet is physically reinforced at the point of authentication, and the reinforcement
is visible as a band of doubled material along the foot with slots punched in it.
Everything above the plica is text; the plica itself is structural and carries no
words.

WHAT WE TAKE. A footer band, doubled tone, no type in it except the countersignature,
with two punched slots rendered as small dark slits. The seal sits below the band and
appears to hang from it. This gives our footer a job. Most web certificates float a
seal in whitespace; ours is anchored to a strip that exists to hold it.

WHAT WE DISCARD. Wax, cords, colour, ribbon. The slots and the doubled band are
enough.

Rights. Wikipedia CC BY-SA. Technique only.

## 5. Letters patent under the Great Seal of Ireland, C 106/153

Issuer: Crown, reign of Charles II
Date: 1670s
Link: https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/explore-the-collection/stories/letter-patent-with-the-great-seal-of-ireland/

What it is. An original grant of land to John Farrell, stating location, terms and
rents payable to the Crown. It survived because it was filed as evidence in a
Chancery hearing in the early eighteenth century and never collected.

Technique or layout move. Two things. The opening initial C is a large engraved
compartment containing a portrait, so the first letter of the document is a picture
frame and the text flows from inside it. And the seal hangs on red and white cord,
two colours twisted, not one. The National Archives note that most surviving letters
patent are clerical copies on the patent rolls with none of this, which is the point:
the decorated original and the registry copy are different objects, and only the
original carries the ornament.

WHAT WE TAKE. The engraved initial as a compartment. Our certificate's first letter
is a box, roughly 64 by 64, holding the one bold engraved element the brief allows,
and the opening line of body text wraps around it. That satisfies the single bold
element rule by putting the engraving where a registry would actually have put it,
at the head of the grant, rather than as a hero image. Also take the two colour twist
for any cord or rule we draw, brown black and the red orange laid alternately.

WHAT WE DISCARD. The portrait, the harp, the horseman, all of it. And we never render
a monarch.

Rights. Open Government Licence v3.0. Safe to quote and describe. Still no image.

## 6. Glasgow MA parchment with the seal in its original metal casing

Issuer: University of Glasgow
Date: circa 1772, MA conferred on Henry Stevenson of Dunlop
Link: https://www.gla.ac.uk/news/archiveofnews/2003/february/headline_29457_en.html

What it is. A degree parchment, handwritten in Latin, signed by named professors
including Thomas Reid and John Millar. Recovered by a furniture removal worker in
Oxford and given to Glasgow University Archives in 2003.

Technique or layout move. The wax seal is intact inside its original metal casing, a
skippet. The seal is not exposed on the face of the document; it is enclosed in a
hard container that hangs from it. Also note the signature block: this is not one
authorising signature, it is a row of named office holders, each signing in his own
capacity, and the document is valid because of the combination.

WHAT WE TAKE. The row of countersignatures. Our certificate carries three signature
slots on one line, each with a printed rule beneath it and a printed capacity below
the rule in small caps, for example Clerk, Examiner, Keeper of the Register. Only one
is ever filled. The other two rules stay blank and that is correct, because a blank
signature line on a printed form reads as an office that exists and was simply not
required today. This is the single cheapest way to make a page feel institutional.

WHAT WE DISCARD. Latin, calligraphy, sheepskin texture, and the skippet, which does
not translate to screen.

Rights. University of Glasgow news item. Describe, cite, do not reproduce.

## 7. Cheque book counterfoil, Drummonds Bank, NT 1256569.1

Issuer: Drummonds Bank, held in the Bankes collection at Kingston Lacy
Date: 15 February to 8 September 1898, cheques DE 90801 to DE 90900
Link: https://www.nationaltrustcollections.org.uk/object/1256569.1

What it is. Not a cheque. The stub. A paper document 85mm by 80mm that is what
remained in the book after a hundred cheques were torn out and spent. The National
Trust catalogued and accessioned the stub as an object in its own right.

Technique or layout move. The stub is almost square, much narrower than the cheque it
came from, and it carries the serial range rather than any single value. A museum
decided the surviving half worth keeping was the half the bank kept, not the half the
money travelled on. The stub is the record; the cheque was consumable.

WHAT WE TAKE. Put a stub on our certificate. A vertical strip on the left, about
110px wide, separated by a perforation line, carrying the file number, the date of
issue and one ruled blank. Then state in the smallest type on the page that this
portion is retained. The user's certificate visually includes the part that does not
belong to them, which is the exact emotional difference between a banknote and a
receipt from an office.

WHAT WE DISCARD. Any amount, any currency, any bank name.

Rights. National Trust Collections record, image copyright National Trust. Cite the
record, do not reuse the image.

## 8. The word cheque comes from the counterfoil, and the Bank of England book of forms and counterparts

Issuer: London goldsmith bankers, then the Bank of England
Date: cheque books of 50, 100 and 200 forms introduced 1830; perforated edges and a
stamped account number from 1863; printed bank names with anti forgery swirls from 1764
Link: https://home.barclays/news/2016/08/evolution-of-the-cheque/

What it is. The etymology, which almost nobody knows. "Cheque" originally named the
counterfoil, the retained stub kept as a check against forgery. The instrument is
named after the piece you do not get.

Technique or layout move. The Bank of England's 1830 books were described as forms
and counterparts, bound or stitched, in fixed counts of 50, 100 and 200. The
perforation on a cheque edge from 1863 onward is therefore not decoration, it is
evidence of an origin: this sheet came out of a numbered book with a known capacity,
and somewhere there is a bound remainder with a matching stub.

WHAT WE TAKE. The vocabulary and the count. Print, in the footer, the form's position
in a stock of known size, in registry diction: "Form 3 of the current book." Combined
with the perforation from entry 7, the page states its own provenance without a single
adjective. Also take 1863 as licence for a hard printed serial in the second colour,
set apart from the body type.

WHAT WE DISCARD. MICR, sort codes, the whole automation half of that article, and any
reference to clearing.

Rights. Barclays corporate archive blog. Cite, quote sparingly, no images.

## 9. The crossing, Bills of Exchange Act 1882 sections 76 and 77

Issuer: UK Parliament, consolidating the Crossed Cheques Act 1876
Date: 1882
Link: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Vict/45-46/61/part/III/crossheading/crossed-cheques/enacted/data.xht

What it is. A statute that defines a graphic mark. Section 76(1) makes two parallel
transverse lines across the face of a cheque, with or without the words "not
negotiable", a general crossing. Section 76(2) makes the name of a banker written
across the face a special crossing. Section 77(4) lets the holder add "not
negotiable" to either.

Technique or layout move. This is the only case I found where a legal instrument's
restriction is expressed as two diagonal rules drawn straight over the document's own
face, deliberately obscuring part of it, with a short phrase written between them.
The mark is applied after issue, by the holder, on top of finished printing, and it
subtracts rights rather than granting them.

WHAT WE TAKE. The crossing as a device, with our own words between the rules. Two
parallel rules at roughly 20 degrees across the upper right corner of the certificate,
in the second colour, with a short phrase set between them in small caps. The phrase
must come from C2, but the shape to hold is exactly two lines with three or four words
trapped between them, drawn over the top of the type beneath. It is the most legible
possible way to say a document confers nothing while still being a document.

WHAT WE DISCARD. The words "not negotiable" themselves, which are too close to
financial claim language for this project, and the banker's name variant.

Rights. Open Government Licence. Statute text is quotable.

## 10. Postal order counterfoils, kept by the office and almost never surviving

Issuer: British Post Office and colonial postal administrations
Date: British postal orders from 1 January 1881; the South African Republic examples
survive from books taken when post offices were captured in the Second Boer War
Links: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postal_orders_of_the_South_African_Republic
https://www.postalmuseum.org/blog/140-years-of-postal-orders/

What it is. The postal order counterfoil stayed in the book at the counter. An issued
order in a collector's hands has no counterfoil, by design. The only known examples
with counterfoils attached come from unissued books removed from captured post
offices during wartime, which means the intact pair exists only where the office
itself stopped functioning.

Technique or layout move. The strongest single fact in this entire file. The two
halves of the transaction were never in the same place after issue. The holder's half
was mobile and spendable; the office's half was immobile and evidentiary. Reuniting
them required the collapse of the office.

WHAT WE TAKE. The line of copy that makes our stub make sense. Print on the stub, in
the smallest type, that this portion is retained by the office, then let the user look
at the fact that they are holding both halves. That is the whole joke, delivered
without a single word of joke telling: the office is unofficial, so nothing was
retained, so you got the register copy too. It suits the brief's dry humour exactly
and it never mentions the protocol.

WHAT WE DISCARD. Poundage, denominations, added postage stamps, and any war story.

Rights. Wikipedia CC BY-SA. Postal Museum blog, cite only, the page refused automated
fetch so treat its detail as unverified until someone opens it by hand.

## 11. Edmondson railway ticket and the ticket dating press

Issuer: Thomas Edmondson; machine held by the Science Museum Group at the National
Railway Museum
Date: system devised at Milton, patented 1840; machine catalogued as object 1869-76;
last Edmondson press switched off 1988
Links: https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co26723/edmondson-ticket-printing-machine
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmondson_railway_ticket

What it is. Card tickets, printed and consecutively numbered by machine, 200 a minute,
numbered 0000 to 9999 by two wheels each running 00 to 99. Over 500 million tickets a
year in Britain by the 1870s.

Technique or layout move. Two separate machines produced the finished ticket, and this
is the move. The press printed and numbered the blank in the works, in bulk, months
before use. The dating press sat in the booking office and impressed the date onto the
card at the moment of sale. A valid ticket therefore carries two different kinds of
mark from two different places, and the date is always the cruder of the two.
Wikipedia also records that Edmondson's serial numbered tickets predate serial
numbering on banknotes by several years, which means the numbered ticket is the older
idea and the numbered note is the imitation.

WHAT WE TAKE. Separate the two marks visibly. The certificate's fixed printing is
crisp and aligned to the grid. The date and the file number are the booking office
layer: set them in a different face, one or two degrees off horizontal, slightly
heavier and slightly blotted, as if impressed onto a blank that was already printed.
Nothing else on the page is allowed to be off axis.

WHAT WE DISCARD. Card stock rendering, colour coded classes, and railway anything.

Rights. Science Museum Group: title, date and maker fields CC0, descriptive text
CC BY 4.0. Safe to cite and describe. Wikipedia CC BY-SA.

## 12. Printed bill of lading, brig Second Attempt, 1811

Issuer: James W. Murdock, Havana to Philadelphia, 93 bags of coffee. Barry-Hayes
Papers, Independence Seaport Museum, digitised by Villanova
Date: 15 June 1811
Link: https://digital.library.villanova.edu/Record/vudl:323549/Details

What it is. A commercial form where the boilerplate is printed and everything that
matters is written in by hand into gaps left for it. It opens with the fixed phrase
"Shipped in good order and well conditioned by", and the shipper's name is the first
handwritten thing on the sheet.

Technique or layout move. The printed sentence runs continuously and the manuscript
fills its holes, so a single line of prose alternates typeface and handwriting three
or four times. The reader parses it as one sentence and simultaneously sees which
facts the office guaranteed and which facts the clerk was told. Blanks are sized to
the expected content, so a short blank means a short answer was expected, and a clerk
writing past the end of a blank is visibly overrunning the form.

WHAT WE TAKE. Set the certificate's central grant as one continuous printed sentence
with the user's string dropped into an inline slot inside it, not on its own line and
not in a box. The slot is underlined, sized to a fixed width, and the string is
rendered in the second, hand adjacent face. If the string is short it does not fill
the rule and the empty remainder of the rule stays visible. If it is long it is
tracked tighter rather than the rule growing. The form does not adapt to the applicant.

WHAT WE DISCARD. Maritime language, cargo, ports, and the phrase itself.

Rights. Villanova Digital Library record, original at Independence Seaport Museum.
Cite the record. Check rights before any image use, which we are not doing anyway.

## 13. Blank form for a demand of payment of a promissory note

Issuer: printed at Washington, Library of Congress Printed Ephemera Collection,
Portfolio 193, Folder 28a
Date: 1826
Link: https://www.loc.gov/item/rbpe.1930280a/

What it is. A pre printed form for a notary to record that he presented a note, that
payment was refused, and that he therefore protested it. The failure was routine
enough to be worth setting in type in advance.

Technique or layout move. Protest wording is fixed and formulaic, ending on the
construction "In testimony whereof, I have hereunto set my hand, and affixed my seal
notarial." The interesting property is that the form pre exists the event. Someone
printed a stack of documents describing a refusal that had not happened yet, leaving
blanks for whose refusal it would turn out to be. The document is a template for bad
news, and its authority comes from being on the shelf before the fact.

WHAT WE TAKE. The closing formula shape. End our certificate with a fixed testimonial
clause in one line of running text, first person, from the office, immediately above
the countersignature rules. Not a tagline and not a sign off, a clause that reads as
the last sentence of a legal instrument. Also take the underlying idea for Window 2:
the examination stamp should look like it was printed before the user took the
examination, because it was.

WHAT WE DISCARD. Notaries, dishonour, protest, non payment, and anything that reads
as a financial failure. The tone we want is procedural, not adverse.

Rights. Library of Congress digital collection. The site refused automated fetch, so
confirm the rights and reproduction statement on the item page by hand before use.

## 14. Sun Fire Office policy registers, the schedule block and the renewal number

Issuer: Sun Fire Office. Core collection on deposit at London Metropolitan Archives,
around 1,200 volumes covering 1710 to 1863
Date: 1710 onward; the indexed 1777 to 1786 slice is searchable via London Lives
Link: https://www.thelondonarchives.org/your-research/research-guides/fire-insurance-records

What it is. Registers of fire insurance policies. Each entry carries policy number,
agent and agency location, the holder's name, status, occupation and address, the
tenants where relevant, the location, type, construction and value of the property,
the premium, the renewal date, and an indication of endorsements.

Technique or layout move. That list is a schedule block, and its order is fixed. The
same categories appear in the same sequence in every entry regardless of whether the
entry has anything to put in them. Sun policies were renewed after five years and a
renewed policy got a wholly new number, so the number identifies a five year term of
a relationship rather than a person, and continuity is expressed by the register
rather than by the document.

WHAT WE TAKE. A schedule of what the certificate grants, set as a ruled block of six
to eight rows, label column left at a fixed width, value column right, hairline rule
between every row and no vertical rules at all. Rows with nothing in them show a
centred en space or a single dash and are never removed. A schedule that still lists
the fields it could not fill is the clearest possible signal that a form was completed
rather than composed. This is the single most useful layout unit in the whole file and
it should sit directly under the grant sentence.

WHAT WE DISCARD. Premiums, values, sums insured, firemarks, and the five year term.
Our certificate does not expire.

# CHIEF READ THIS FIRST

## The five strongest moves, ranked

1. The chirograph cut. Give the certificate a wavy top edge produced by an SVG clip
path, and run a line of the office's own text along that edge so the letters are
sliced across the middle. There is a counterpart somewhere with the other halves.
This is authentication by geometry, it is free to render, and no crypto site has ever
done it. Source, entry 3.

2. The retained stub. A perforated vertical strip down the left carrying the file
number and the date, with one line of tiny type saying the portion is retained by the
office. The National Trust accessioned a Drummonds stub as an object; the Post Office
kept the counterfoil and the holder never saw it again. Sources, entries 7 and 10.

3. The schedule block. Six to eight ruled rows, fixed label column, hairline rules,
empty rows left visibly empty. This is what makes the page read as completed rather
than designed, and it is where "Confers nothing" can live as a row value rather than
as a slogan. Source, entry 14.

4. The two layer date. Fixed printing crisp and on grid; the file number and date
impressed at a degree off axis in a heavier face, as if a booking clerk put them on a
blank that was printed months earlier. Nothing else on the page breaks alignment.
Source, entry 11.

5. The blind seal and the applied patch. An ink free embossed seal drawn as shadow and
highlight only, plus one small applied patch of different paper with two fasteners for
the examination stamp, so the Window 2 mark is physically a later addition by another
hand. Sources, entries 2 and 4.

## The one structural idea

Our certificate should be built as half of a pair. Every reference in this file that
felt genuinely institutional, rather than merely old, turned out to work the same way:
the document in your hand is the mobile half of something, and the fixed half stayed
in a book at a counter. The chirograph was cut through a word so the halves could
prove each other. The cheque is named after the stub it was torn from, not after
itself. The postal order counterfoil stayed in the office so completely that surviving
attached pairs exist only where the office was overrun. This is the actual difference
between currency and office paper, and it is not a matter of ornament. Currency is
whole, self sufficient and anonymous, because it has to work in the hand of a stranger
with no registry behind it. Office paper is a fragment that only means something
because a matching fragment is filed somewhere with a number on it. So the move is not
to add stamps and seals until the page looks official. It is to make the page
structurally incomplete: a torn edge, a perforation, a retained stub, a schedule with
rows nobody filled, three countersignature rules with one signature on them. Then let
the joke land on its own, which is that this office is unofficial, so no counterpart
was ever filed, and the visitor is holding both halves of a record that exists nowhere
else. That reads as a gift from someone who understands registries, and it could never
be mistaken for someone farming a brand.
