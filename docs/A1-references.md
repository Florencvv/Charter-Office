# A1 REFERENCES. Construction logic from real financial paper.
Compiled by A1, banknote historian. For the front end engineer building the
engraving in canvas and SVG.

No image of a real note goes in our file. Everything below is construction
logic. Where a source and a second source disagree, or where I could not
confirm a position from a written description, the line says "unverified"
rather than guessing.

A note on how I read positions. Museum and catalogue records transcribe
lettering in reading order, which gives a reliable vertical stack even when the
record never says "top left". Where a position comes from reading order rather
than an explicit statement, I say so.


## 1. National Bank Note, Series of 1902 Plain Back, 10 Dollars

Issuer: The First National Bank of Indiana, Indiana, Pennsylvania. Charter
number issued by the Comptroller of the Currency. Note dated Feb. 25, 1903.
Link: https://en.numista.com/catalogue/note248604.html

Object. A large size US banknote, 187 by 79 mm, printed by the Bureau of
Engraving and Printing but carrying a private bank's name as the promisor.
The federal government printed it, one named bank owed on it, and both facts
are stated on the same face.

TECHNIQUE BREAKDOWN.
- The face is a stack of ten text fields in strict reading order, confirmed by
  the catalogue transcription: authority heading, security clause, country,
  series line, two federal signatures with their titles, issuing bank name,
  obligation verb, amount in words, place and date, two bank officer
  signatures. Every field is one line. Nothing wraps.
- The issuing bank's name is the largest lettering on the sheet after the
  amount. It is set in shaded type, meaning engraved roman capitals carrying a
  cut drop shadow on one side, so the letters read as raised metal rather than
  as flat ink (Wikipedia, National Bank Note, describes "prominent shaded type
  in the middle"). Exact typeface class unverified beyond "shaded".
- Place and date sit on a single line under the amount, in three registers:
  town in upper and lower case, state in full capitals, then a full calendar
  date. Reproduce as one baseline, three type treatments, comma separated.
  Example structure: "Indiana, PENNSYLVANIA Feb. 25, 1903".
- The date is not the bank's charter date and not the issue date. Its meaning
  was never established (Friedberg, Paper Money of the United States, Part 3).
  Take the visual convention, do not claim the date means anything.
- The portrait sits in an oval at the left third, facing inward toward the
  text. The Treasury seal sits to the right of centre. One bank officer
  signature sits directly below the seal (Intermountain Histories, 1902 five
  dollar note record).
- The charter number is overprinted on the face in the same ink as the seal,
  red for the 1902 first issue, blue thereafter. From about 1902 to 1924 a
  single geographic capital letter was printed twice on the face, each time
  next to a charter number and in the same ink, which fixes the count of
  overprinted charter numbers on the face at two. Wikipedia adds that on large
  size notes a charter number was also positioned inside the engraved border.
  Total count on the 1902 face is therefore two overprinted plus at least one
  engraved. Exact engraved count unverified.

LAYOUT. Treat the face as a 12 by 5 grid on a 2.37:1 sheet.
- Columns 1 to 4, rows 2 to 4: portrait oval, inset from the border by about
  one twelfth of the width.
- Columns 4 to 12, row 1: authority heading and security clause, centred,
  small caps, two lines.
- Columns 4 to 10, row 3: bank name, the largest text block.
- Columns 4 to 10, row 4: obligation verb, then amount in words.
- Columns 4 to 10, row 5: place, state, date on one baseline.
- Columns 9 to 12, rows 2 to 4: seal, with the overprinted charter number
  above it and one signature under it.
- Row 5, left and right ends: the two bank officer signatures, titles set
  under short rules.

WHAT WE TAKE. The ten field stack in that exact order, and the rule that the
issuer's own name is the largest type on the sheet, larger than the authority
that printed it. WHAT WE DISCARD. The portrait, the seal of a real state, and
any wording that promises payment.

Rights. The note design is US federal work of the 1900s and long out of
copyright. The Numista catalogue page is a description, not an image we copy.
We lift structure only.


## 2. National Bank Note, Series of 1882 Brown Back, 5 Dollars

Issuer: The National Bank of Fayetteville, Fayetteville, North Carolina.
Note dated Dec. 12, 1900. Designer Geo. W. Casilear, engraver Alonzo C. Hatch.
Link: https://en.numista.com/catalogue/note213794.html

Object. A large size note whose back is built around the bank's charter number
rather than around a picture. The number is the subject of the reverse.

TECHNIQUE BREAKDOWN.
- The back is a three panel composition on one horizontal axis. Left oval
  holds the state seal of the bank's state. Right oval holds an American
  eagle. The centre holds the charter number, set large, surrounded by
  ornament (Numista reverse description).
- Two ink pass construction. The charter number and the ornament immediately
  around it print in green. Everything else on the back prints in brown
  (Friedberg, Part 3, repeated for every denomination in the series). This is
  a second colour used to isolate one field, not to decorate the whole sheet.
- The face carries the charter number in several places (Numista notes it
  "appears in several locations"). Exact count and position unverified.
- Sheet position is encoded as a script check letter A to D, printed twice on
  the face. All four notes on a sheet carry identical serial numbers and
  differ only by that letter and by denomination (Friedberg, Part 3).
- Two unlike serial numbers appear on the face of every national bank note.
  One is the Treasury number, the running count of sheets printed nationwide.
  One is the bank number, the running count for that bank alone. After
  22 August 1925 the Treasury number was dropped and replaced by a duplicate
  of the bank number.

LAYOUT. Back reads as a 3 by 1 band, roughly 25 / 50 / 25 of the width, with
the two ovals vertically centred and the number block filling the middle
half. Statutory text runs as a fine line block under the centre panel.

WHAT WE TAKE. The centre panel that treats the number itself as the picture,
and the two ink rule where the second colour touches only the number and its
immediate ornament. WHAT WE DISCARD. The eagle, the state seal, and the
receivability clause.

Rights. Public domain by age. Structure only.


## 3. National Bank Note, Original Series, charter number over and under seal

Issuer: various national banks, 1 and 2 dollar notes delivered 1874.
Link: https://www.numismaticnews.net/paper-money/original-series-charter-number-over-seal-varieties

Object. The moment the charter number first appeared on US paper money, added
by overprint to a plate that had no room designed for it.

TECHNIQUE BREAKDOWN.
- The Act of June 20, 1874 required the charter number on the notes so that
  worn currency could be sorted for redemption. The number was added as an
  overprint, not engraved into the plate.
- On sheets delivered between 12 May and 27 July 1874 the number was printed
  above the Treasury seal. From 28 July 1874 it moved below the seal, because
  unissued older sheets returned for overprinting had no clear space above the
  seal. New production then followed the returned stock for consistency.
- One charter number per note in this phase. It became a two per note field
  only in later series.
- The number was normally printed in red. A small number of five dollar notes
  carried it in black and in script numerals rather than block numerals
  (Friedberg, Part 3).
- Seal shape carries the series: the Original Series seal has rays, the Series
  of 1875 seal has scallops (Friedberg, Part 3, signature tables).

LAYOUT. A single overprint block whose vertical position is determined by
whatever white space the underlying engraving left. The number sits on the
same vertical axis as the seal, immediately above or immediately below it,
never beside it.

WHAT WE TAKE. Charter number locked to the seal's vertical axis, and the idea
that the number is an overprint layer added after the engraving, drawn in a
different colour and slightly out of register. WHAT WE DISCARD. The two
position variety itself, which would read as an error to anyone who does not
know the story.

Rights. Public domain by age. Structure only.


## 4. Treasury engraving specification for the National Currency 20 dollar face, 1864

Issuer: US Treasury contract text, quoted in the reference article.
Link: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_and_engraving_on_United_States_banknotes

Object. The written order that told an engraver how to build a national
currency face. Not a description of a note, but the construction brief for one.

TECHNIQUE BREAKDOWN. Quoted fragments from the contract:
- "there shall be engraved upon the left-hand portion of the note a vignette",
  and "on the opposite, or right-hand end of the note" a second design. Two
  pictorial anchors, one at each end.
- "Between these two vignettes shall be engraved two legends". The text lives
  in the gap between the two pictures.
- "In the upper right-hand corner of the note the figure 20 is to be engraved,
  of suitable size, in a white letter with black shade". A reversed numeral,
  white on a shaded ground, in one named corner.
- "The words 'First National Bank' shall be engraved above the central
  vignette". Bank name above the central element.
- "The corners shall be filled with proper counters, indicating the
  denomination of the note, and the interstices be filled with work of a
  character to add as much as practicable to the security of the note against
  counterfeiting; the whole to be surrounded by a suitable border".
- The same article quotes the plate order: "twelve (12) transfers therefrom
  shall be made upon separate plates of steel". One die, twelve plates.
- I could not confirm the full unbroken contract text from a second source.
  Treat the fragments as quoted and the joins between them as unverified.

LAYOUT. The specification is itself the layout: four corner counters, a border
enclosing everything, a left vignette, a right vignette, legends in the
central gap, a shaded reversed numeral in the upper right corner, and every
remaining gap filled with security work.

WHAT WE TAKE. Two rules. First, counters go in all four corners and carry the
identifying number, so our charter number occupies four corner counters.
Second, the interstice rule: after the named fields are placed, every leftover
gap gets filled with fine work, because empty space is where a forger works.
WHAT WE DISCARD. The pictorial vignettes at both ends. We have one engraving
and it is not a picture of a person.

Rights. US government contract text of 1864, public domain. Quoted here for
construction logic.


## 5. National Bank Note, Series of 1929 small size, 10 Dollars

Issuer: The First National Bank of Saltsburg, Pennsylvania. Series of 1929.
Link: https://en.numista.com/catalogue/note207678.html

Object. The last national bank notes, reduced to 156 by 66 mm, with the bank's
identity demoted from engraved plate work to overprint.

TECHNIQUE BREAKDOWN.
- Four signatures, four corners of the text field. Register of the Treasury
  upper left, Treasurer of the United States upper right, Cashier lower left,
  President lower right (Numista obverse description). Federal authority on
  top, local authority underneath, on both sides.
- The seal sits to the right of the central portrait.
- The bank name is no longer engraved into the plate. It is overstamped in
  black ink just above the engraved lettering (Wikipedia, National Bank Note).
  The charter number likewise leaves the engraved border and becomes pure
  overprint.
- Type 1, printed 1929 to 1933, carries two charter numbers, one in black at
  each end of the note. Type 2, printed 1933 to 1935, carries four: the same
  two in black plus two more in brown printed next to the serial numbers
  (uspapermoney.info, details of serial numbering; Numista confirms two versus
  four).
- Type 1 serial numbers run six digits with a letter at each end, for example
  A012345A. Type 2 drops the trailing letter.
- National currency used no star notes. A spoiled note was replaced by
  reprinting the identical serial number, letters included.

LAYOUT. A 156 by 66 field with the portrait centred, the obligation split into
a left block and a right block flanking it, and the four signatures pinned to
the four corners of the inner text area rather than to the sheet corners.

WHAT WE TAKE. The four corner signature arrangement, authority above and local
below on each side. This is the single cleanest field layout in the whole set.
WHAT WE DISCARD. The overstamped bank name, which looks cheap next to the
engraved bank names of the large size notes.

Rights. Public domain by age. Structure only.


## 6. Silver Certificate backs of 1886 and 1891, geometric lathe work

Issuer: US Treasury. Reference article by the American Numismatic Association.
Link: https://blog.money.org/coin-collecting/abstractions-of-value

Object. Banknote backs made entirely of machine ornament with no picture at
all. The closest historical thing to what a canvas routine can actually draw.

TECHNIQUE BREAKDOWN.
- The geometric lathe was programmed by "calculating the pitch and frequency of
  a single line, which was rotated and offset in such a way that it returned to
  the point of origin after completing a set number of cycles". That is a
  closed parametric curve. If the cycle count does not close, the pattern is
  wrong.
- Operators chose cogs of given sizes and built them into gear trains. Ratios
  between the gears set the pattern. Bad ratios gave a visually dissonant
  result, good ratios gave a repeating wave.
- Named ornament types, and these are the words to use in the code:
  rosette, a concentric accumulation of waves or petals in an elliptical
  arrangement; counter, a denomination or number sitting inside a machined
  rosette; guilloche, interlacing bands; bond tint, a background of concentric
  lines used on bonds and certificates; straight line ruling, engraving laid
  in parallel rows.
- Transfer allowed a single rosette to be "subdivided and the fragments
  repeated, reversed and mirrored" to build extensible shapes. So the correct
  way to code a border is to draw one unit, then mirror and repeat it, never
  to draw the whole border.
- The article puts these abstractions on the backs of the large size Silver
  Certificates of 1886 and 1891, and on tax stamps, lottery tickets, diplomas
  and commercial documents. Same vocabulary, different paper.
- The US Bureau's lathe is described as a ten wheel system with settings known
  only to its operator. The ten wheel claim is repeated by the Mathpuzzle
  column below but I found no primary source, so treat it as unverified.

LAYOUT. A back built from a central counter in a rosette, flanked by mirrored
fragments of that same rosette, all sitting on a bond tint of concentric
lines, with straight line ruling filling the outer margins.

WHAT WE TAKE. The counter in a rosette, holding our charter number, and the
one unit plus mirror plus repeat rule for the whole border. WHAT WE DISCARD.
The full field of lathe work. Our brief allows one bold element, so the lathe
work is the engraving and nothing else competes with it.

Rights. The note designs are public domain by age. The article is a secondary
source we read, not copy. Structure only.


## 7. Guilloche mathematics, the codeable version

Reference: Ed Pegg Jr, Math Games column, guilloche patterns.
Link: https://www.mathpuzzle.com/MAA/13-Guilloche%20Patterns/mathgames_02_09_04.html

Object. The maths behind the machine ornament, written as functions rather
than as gear trains.

TECHNIQUE BREAKDOWN.
- The curves belong to the roulette family, generated by a wheel rolling
  within or around a wheel while a pen traces from a fixed offset. The column
  names epitrochoids specifically. Wikipedia's guilloche article names the
  hypotrochoid, the spirograph case.
- The parametric form to implement: for a fixed circle radius R, a rolling
  circle radius r, and a pen offset d, the hypotrochoid is
  x = (R - r) cos t + d cos(((R - r) / r) t),
  y = (R - r) sin t - d sin(((R - r) / r) t).
  Swap the signs to get the epitrochoid. The pattern closes when R / r is
  rational; the number of petals is the numerator in lowest terms. Note that
  the column itself does not print these equations, so treat the equations as
  standard textbook forms I supplied, not as a quotation.
- The column does print one working three wheel formula for a computer drawn
  guilloche band, with a, b as frequency ratios, c, d, e, f as phase and
  amplitude offsets, and a run index n from 0 to 19 producing twenty
  superimposed curves. The important structural fact is the count: a band is
  about twenty stacked curves, not one.
- A band is therefore built by drawing the same curve twenty times with the
  phase term stepped, giving the moire that reads as engraving.

LAYOUT. Not a layout. Two primitives for the engineer. A rosette is one closed
roulette drawn N times with stepped phase. A band is the same thing swept
along a path.

WHAT WE TAKE. The twenty pass stacking rule and the rational ratio closure
rule. Both are cheap in canvas and both are what makes a curve read as
security printing instead of as clip art. WHAT WE DISCARD. Any attempt to
reproduce a specific historical rosette. We are generating, not copying.

Rights. Mathematics is not copyrightable. We are not reproducing the article.


## 8. Siderography, the transfer roll

Reference: Jacob Perkins, from about 1805.
Link: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siderography

Object. The process that made identical repetition of an engraved unit
possible, and therefore the reason nineteenth century security paper looks the
way it does.

TECHNIQUE BREAKDOWN.
- An engraver cuts one unit into soft steel. The die is hardened. A soft steel
  cylinder is rolled under pressure across the hardened die, taking the design
  in relief. That roll is hardened in turn. The roll is then rocked across a
  soft plate as many times as needed, laying the same unit down repeatedly in
  perfect register. Wikipedia states only the plate to cylinder transfer step;
  the full four step sequence is standard printing history and is marked here
  as unverified against a single source.
- Consequence for us: a nineteenth century sheet is not drawn, it is
  assembled from a small library of repeated units. Borders repeat a unit.
  Counters repeat a unit. The same vignette appears on a banknote, a stock
  certificate and a bond.
- Steel rather than copper is the reason. Steel plates survived long runs;
  a hardened plate could yield very large numbers of impressions.
- The associated numbers cited in secondary sources, 240 impressions
  transferred to a printing plate and 400,000 imprints from a plate, come from
  a single collector source and are unverified.

LAYOUT. No layout. A construction rule.

WHAT WE TAKE. Build one SVG symbol per ornament unit and instance it. If a
shape appears twice on our sheet it must be the same shape, pixel for pixel,
because that is what a transfer roll does. Hand drawn variation is the tell of
a fake. WHAT WE DISCARD. Nothing. This one is free.

Rights. Historical process. Structure only.


## 9. Educational Series Silver Certificate, 1 Dollar, 1896

Issuer: US Treasury. Design by Will Hicok Low, face engraved by Charles
Schlecht. Design accepted 10 July 1894, printing began 18 April 1896.
Link: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_Instructing_Youth

Object. A US note whose border is a list. Twenty three personal names sit in
wreaths around the edge of the face.

TECHNIQUE BREAKDOWN.
- The border is not abstract ornament. It is a sequence of 23 wreath cartouches
  running around the face, each holding one surname in capitals. That converts
  the border from decoration into a register of names.
- Each wreath is a repeated engraved unit; only the name inside changes. This
  is the transfer roll logic of reference 8 applied to a text field.
- The centre holds an allegorical scene with an open book carrying real text,
  the Constitution, so the note contains a document within a document.
- Sequence of names as listed: Longfellow, Sherman, Lincoln, Irving, Cooper,
  Fulton, Calhoun, Clay, Jackson, Adams, Jefferson, Washington, Franklin,
  Hamilton, Perry, Marshall, Webster, Morse, Hawthorne, Bancroft, Grant,
  Farragut, Emerson. Their exact clockwise positions are unverified.

LAYOUT. A rectangular ring of 23 evenly spaced cartouches, with the corner
positions taken by larger units, enclosing a central pictorial field that
occupies roughly the middle 60 percent of the width and 70 percent of the
height.

WHAT WE TAKE. One move. A border whose repeating unit carries readable text
rather than pattern. For us that unit can carry the charter number, repeated
around the edge, so the number becomes the ornament. WHAT WE DISCARD. The
allegorical centre and the name list.

Rights. Public domain by age. Structure only.


## 10. State Credit Note, 500 Roubles, Russian Empire, 1912

Issuer: State Bank of the Russian Empire. Designer Rihards Zarins. Printed by
the Expedition for the Preparation of Government Papers. Issued 1913.
Link: https://www.worldbanknotescoins.com/2017/12/russia-500-rubles-banknote-1912-emperor-peter-the-great.html
Process reference: https://cmykhistory.com/ivan-orloff-and-his-press/

Object. A very large note, 260 by 122 mm, printed by the Orlov process, which
puts several colours down in one impression with no register error.

TECHNIQUE BREAKDOWN.
- Orlov press mechanics. Several colour formes sit on a rotating cylinder.
  "At one revolution of the forme cylinder each forme or block receives its
  requisite supply of ink from its own rollers." Each forme hands its colour to
  a shared composition roller, which then transfers the whole design to paper
  in one action. Registration is exact because the paper is struck once.
- Orlov's working rule: inks must be laid strongest tack first, weakest last,
  because "the color of less strength passes over to that of greater strength".
- The visible result is a single engraved line that changes colour along its
  length with no gap and no overlap. That is the effect to reproduce, and in
  SVG it is a gradient applied along a stroke, not two stacked strokes.
- By 1897 the Russian government ran 32 such presses.
- On the note itself, a guilloche rosette holding the face value 500 sits at
  the lower left of the face. On the back the denomination sits at the centre
  inside a burst of sun flames. Both are counters inside ornament, the same
  move as reference 6.
- The watermark is a portrait, not a pattern.

LAYOUT. Face reads as a large left field carrying the state arms and the
statutory text, a lower left rosette counter, and a relief panel above the
centre holding a large numeral and the year. Positions beyond those named in
the catalogue record are unverified.

WHAT WE TAKE. Colour changing along a single stroke, done as a stroke gradient
so it reads as one printing pass rather than two. WHAT WE DISCARD. The
imperial arms, the crown, the portrait and the full rainbow underprint, which
would fight the one bold element rule.

Rights. Public domain by age. We take the printing effect, not the artwork.


## 11. Assignat, French Republic, 1790s

Issuer: French revolutionary government. Reference: Citeco, on an original
assignat press.
Link: https://www.citeco.fr/en/assignat-press-french-revolution

Object. Government paper backed by confiscated land, produced under pressure
in enormous quantity, with authentication bolted on rather than designed in.

TECHNIQUE BREAKDOWN.
- Authentication was a stack of separate layers, not one integrated design:
  a watermark in the paper, an embossed dry seal struck identically on front
  and back, an individual number on every note, and hand signatures that
  changed between issues. The dry seal is described elsewhere as eight sided;
  that shape is unverified here.
- The number is the primary authentication, applied per note. Manual numbering
  by the treasury was too slow, so from 1792 the process was automated, and
  Richer's numbering machine, fitted to the press itself, came into use in
  October 1793.
- Production moved from moveable type to stereotype plates cast from a matrix
  carrying the whole assignat image, which removed typesetting from the loop.
  Wooden platens were replaced with metal ones, cutting the number of
  impressions needed.
- Higher denominations of 200, 300 and 1000 livres carried detachable coupons
  (American Numismatic Association). This is the counterfoil vocabulary
  appearing on currency rather than on a bond.
- Margins carried the republican watchwords as running text.
- A counterfeiting warning naming the death penalty appears on some issues.

LAYOUT. A tall portrait format sheet, text dominant, with the number placed by
machine rather than composed into the design, and therefore sitting slightly
askew relative to the printed frame.

WHAT WE TAKE. Two moves. The number applied as a separate mechanical layer,
visibly not part of the engraving, and the embossed dry seal rendered as a
colourless relief, which in SVG is an inset and drop shadow pair with no fill.
WHAT WE DISCARD. The revolutionary slogans and the death penalty line.

Rights. Public domain by age. Structure only.


## 12. Bank of England early notes and the white note

Issuer: Bank of England. Reference: Bank of England Museum, early banknotes.
Link: https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/museum/online-collections/banknotes/early-banknotes

Object. Black ink on white paper, printed on one side only, mostly text, with
a single small ornament. The quietest security document in this list.

TECHNIQUE BREAKDOWN.
- The only ornament is a Britannia medallion in a circular frame, derived from
  the Bank's own seal. One mark, small, and no competition for it anywhere on
  the sheet.
- The printed sum block, introduced by 1743, sets the denomination in white
  letters on a solid black ground. A reversed panel used as the value counter.
  This is the same reversal the 1864 US contract asks for in reference 4.
- Early notes are part printed and completed by hand. An example of 1699 is
  printed with the medallion and the promise to pay, with everything else
  written in. A 1732 fifty pound note is part printed and completed by hand
  with further notes on the back.
- The 1855 redesign gave a Britannia in the manner of a Saxon princess,
  engraved by Daniel Maclise. The single sided fully printed white note stayed
  in circulation until 1957.
- The watermark carries the bank name, the value and the chief cashier's
  signature. Authentication sits inside the paper rather than on it.

LAYOUT. A tall sheet. Medallion small and high on the left. Promise to pay as
a single long line across the upper middle. Sum block low left. Number, date
and signature filled in below. Very large white margins.

WHAT WE TAKE. Two moves. The reversed sum block, white type on solid ground,
used as the counter for our charter number. And the part printed and completed
by hand convention, where the generated fields are set in a different weight
and colour from the fixed engraved text, so a reader can see which parts of
the certificate were filled in. WHAT WE DISCARD. Britannia, and the single
sided emptiness, which would read as unfinished on a screen.

Rights. The historic designs are public domain by age. The Bank's museum page
is a source we read. Structure only.


## 13. Paper Money of the United States, Part 3, National Bank Notes

Reference: Friedberg catalogue text, national bank notes section, hosted as a
PDF. Read in full for this brief.
Link: https://neilsberman.com/currency/PaperMoneyoftheUS_Part3_NationalBankNotes.pdf

Object. The catalogue text that states, in one paragraph, exactly which fields
on a national bank note varied between banks. That paragraph is our whole
project.

TECHNIQUE BREAKDOWN.
- "The basic designs on the National Bank Notes are the same for all banks.
  The only differences are in the name of the bank, the bank charter number,
  the bank signatures and the coat of arms of the state in which the bank was
  located." Four variable fields on a fixed plate. Everything else is common.
- Charter numbers ran 1 to 14,348, allocated in order, so the number encodes
  the year of chartering. Charter 1 went to the First National Bank of
  Philadelphia in June 1863. The highest number to appear on an issued note
  was 14,320.
- Notes were printed four to a sheet, most often, with identical serial
  numbers across the sheet and a script check letter A to D marking position.
  Usual denomination combinations were 1-1-1-2, 5-5-5-5, 10-10-10-10,
  10-10-10-20, 20-20-20-20, 50-50-50-100 and 50-100.
- Production was split by geography for a decade. Until July 1875 the whole
  note came from private printers in New York. From September 1875 the faces
  came from the Bureau of Engraving and Printing while the backs stayed in New
  York, except the black portion of the five dollar back which went to a
  printer in Washington. All backs moved to Washington in January 1877, and
  the entire job moved there around October 1877.
- Federal signature plates were reused for years after the named officials
  left office, so a note can carry signatures that were engraved decades
  before it was printed. The signature is a plate object, not a record of who
  was in the room.
- Geographic letters N, E, S, M, W and P were printed twice on the face from
  about 1902 to 1924, in the same ink as the charter number, purely to speed
  sorting at redemption.

LAYOUT. Not a layout. The variable field list, which is the model for our own
certificate: fixed engraved plate, four fields that change per issue.

WHAT WE TAKE. The four variable field rule. Our generated certificate should
change exactly four things from the typed string, and everything else should
be identical on every certificate ever issued. WHAT WE DISCARD. The state coat
of arms.

Rights. The note designs are public domain by age. The catalogue text is
quoted in short fragments for construction facts and is not reproduced.


# CHIEF, READ THIS FIRST

Five findings, ranked.

1. A national bank note held only four variable fields on an otherwise
   identical plate, the bank name, the charter number, the bank signatures and
   the state arms, which tells us our certificate must change exactly four
   things and hold everything else fixed.
2. The issuing bank's own name was the largest lettering on the face, set in
   shaded roman capitals with a cut drop shadow, sitting above the obligation
   line and below the federal authority heading, so the private name outranked
   the government that printed the paper.
3. The 1929 notes pin four signatures to the four corners of the text field,
   federal authority on the top two and local officers on the bottom two,
   which is the cleanest signature arrangement in the whole set and costs
   nothing to build.
4. The 1864 Treasury contract orders counters in all four corners carrying the
   denomination and every remaining gap filled with fine work, which gives us
   both the corner treatment for the charter number and the rule that no empty
   space is left inside the border.
5. Lathe ornament was never drawn whole; one rosette was cut once, then
   subdivided, mirrored and repeated by transfer roll, so our border must be
   one SVG symbol instanced many times, and any two identical shapes on the
   sheet must be byte identical.

## Recommended field layout for our certificate

Landscape sheet, ratio 2.37 to 1, matching the 187 by 79 mm large size note.
Grid of 12 columns by 6 rows. Percentages are of sheet width and height.

Fixed frame.
- Border inset 4 percent on all sides. Border is one repeated unit, mirrored
  on the vertical axis and again on the horizontal axis.
- Four corner counters, each an oval lathe rosette about 9 percent of the
  width, each holding the charter number. That is four of the charter number
  appearances.
- Every gap between named fields is filled with fine ruling. No empty ground
  inside the border.

Field stack, in this order, top to bottom, centred on columns 4 to 10.
1. Row 1, y about 10 percent. Authority heading. "THE CHARTER OFFICE" in small
   shaded capitals, letterspaced, the width of columns 4 to 10.
2. Row 1, y about 16 percent. Status line, one line, small caps, carrying the
   word unofficial. This is the position the notes gave the security clause.
3. Row 2, y about 24 percent. Series line, small, in the second colour.
4. Row 3, y about 40 percent. The issued name, taken from the typed string.
   Largest type on the sheet. Shaded roman capitals with a one pixel offset
   drop shadow. This is the field the eye must land on first.
5. Row 4, y about 52 percent. The grant line, one line, sentence case.
6. Row 5, y about 64 percent. Place and date on one baseline, three type
   treatments, comma separated, following "Town, STATE Month Day, Year".
7. Row 6, y about 84 percent. Two signature lines, one at columns 2 to 5, one
   at columns 8 to 11, each a short rule with a title set beneath it in small
   caps. Left is the clerk, right is the registrar.

Left block.
- Columns 1 to 4, rows 2 to 5. The engraving. One rosette, generated from the
  typed string, in an oval about 26 percent of the width. This is the single
  bold element and nothing else on the sheet competes with it.

Right block.
- Columns 9 to 12, rows 2 to 4. The seal, about 15 percent of the width,
  rendered as a colourless embossed relief, inset plus drop shadow, no fill.
- Directly above the seal, on the seal's vertical axis, the charter number
  overprinted in the second colour, slightly out of register with the
  engraving beneath it. That is the fifth appearance of the number.
- The right signature line sits directly beneath the seal.

Colour rule, two passes only.
- Pass one, brown black intaglio: border, rosette, all engraved text, the two
  signature rules.
- Pass two, red orange: the charter number overprint, the series line, and the
  ornament immediately surrounding the corner counters. Nothing else.

Field count check. The charter number appears five times, four in corner
counters and one overprinted above the seal. The name appears once. The
signatures appear twice. That is four variable fields, matching the historical
rule.
